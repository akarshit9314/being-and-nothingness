    Kant’s Categorical Imperative

    1. The Problem Kant Addresses

    Moral philosophy seeks principles that are not dependent on: - personal desires, - cultural traditions, - consequences alone.

    Kant argues that morality must be grounded in reason itself, not in contingent human inclinations.

    2. Hypothetical vs Categorical Imperatives

    An imperative is a command of reason.

    Hypothetical Imperative

    • Form: If you want X, do Y
    • Depends on goals or desires
    • Example:
      If you want to be healthy, exercise.

    Categorical Imperative

    • Form: Do Y
    • Unconditional
    • Binding on all rational beings regardless of desires

    Moral laws, Kant argues, must be categorical.

    3. First Formulation: Universal Law

    “Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.”

    This formulation asks: - What is the principle (maxim) behind my action? - Can I will that everyone act on this principle?

    If universalizing the action leads to contradiction, the action is immoral.

    Example: Lying

    If everyone lied when convenient, trust would collapse, making the practice of lying itself impossible. Thus, lying fails the test of universalization.

    4. Second Formulation: Humanity as an End

    “Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means.”

    This formulation emphasizes: - the intrinsic worth of rational beings, - respect for autonomy and dignity.

    Using someone merely as a means involves manipulation or coercion that bypasses their rational agency.

    5. Third Formulation: Kingdom of Ends

    “Act according to maxims of a universally legislating member of a merely possible kingdom of ends.”

    Here, morality is conceived as: - a system of laws, - legislated by rational agents, - respecting each agent as both lawgiver and subject.

    6. Autonomy and Moral Worth

    For Kant: - An action has moral worth only if done from duty. - Actions done merely in accordance with duty, but motivated by inclination, lack moral worth.

    Moral autonomy means: - giving oneself the law through reason, - not being ruled by desires or external authority.

    7. Strengths of Kantian Ethics

    • Grounds morality in rationality, not emotion or outcomes
    • Respects human dignity and rights
    • Provides clear moral constraints

    8. Major Criticisms

    • Rigidity: ignores consequences in extreme cases
    • Conflicting duties are difficult to resolve
    • Abstract reasoning may overlook social and emotional realities

    9. Philosophical Significance

    Kant’s categorical imperative represents one of the most influential attempts to ground morality in pure practical reason, shaping modern debates on rights, autonomy, and moral law.
